# User Personas

## Overview

Pamoja's target audience is defined by one core dynamic: **merchants pull customers into the app, and customers bring their own social lives with them.** The segmentation dimension is role in the commerce-and-social loop — seller, buyer, cross-border buyer, and the hybrid creator-merchant who embodies the modular channel model.

Personas are Nigerian first because that is the launch beachhead ([[README]] sets the Africa-first architecture; this document names the people who prove it in market one). Each persona carries a tech-comfort rating because it determines onboarding depth, UI complexity, and whether a feature can assume phone-savvy behavior.

## Persona 1: Ada the Market Trader

**Tagline:** The merchant whose whole business runs on WhatsApp status and a notebook — and who will drag her buyers into Pamoja with her.
**Demographic:** 28, female, Lagos (Mainland / market axis), secondary-school educated, runs a women's fashion and ankara shop in Balogun Market with a small supplier network.
**Role:** SME owner / market trader — sole proprietor, does buying, selling, and bookkeeping herself.
**Tech Comfort:** Low–Medium. Proficient with WhatsApp and Instagram status; distrustful of anything that looks like a scam; pays via transfer and OPay.

### Goals
- Sell more without losing track of orders — today she takes orders by WhatsApp message and loses half of them in the chat history.
- Get paid reliably and know who paid, when, and for what.
- Show her stock to buyers who are not on WhatsApp or don't have her number.
- Keep her customers close — repeat buyers are her whole margin.
- Look like a real business (catalog, prices, verified channel) without paying for a website.

### Frustrations
- Orders, payments, and promises scattered across WhatsApp chats, voice notes, and paper.
- Buyers who haggle or "cancel" after she has already set aside stock.
- No record of who owes her — debts get "forgotten."
- Instagram customers find her, but the sale has to move to WhatsApp + bank transfer, and strangers don't trust a transfer to a personal account.
- Cannot get a loan or supplier credit because she has no digital sales record to prove her business.

### Behaviors
- Runs the shop 7 days a week; phones are central to her day but data is expensive — she uses WhatsApp on cheap Android, screenshots product photos, posts on status in the evening.
- Sends "bank alert" screenshots as proof of payment.
- Trusts word-of-mouth and physical networks more than online reputations.
- Rarely installs new apps unless a customer or colleague insists.

### Scenario
Ada gets a WhatsApp order for three ankara dresses from a customer she met at a wedding. She confirms, the customer transfers money, Ada sends a screenshot of the alert as proof — then she loses the chat when she clears her storage. Later the customer says the dresses are the wrong size and wants a refund; Ada has no order record, no proof of the agreed size, and the dispute costs her a regular buyer. A friend shows her Pamoja: she sets up a business channel, uploads her dresses with prices, and shares a link in her WhatsApp status. Her next customer orders in chat, pays in the app, and Ada sees the whole order — product, size, amount, delivery — in one place. She starts sending every buyer her Pamoja link instead of her account number.

## Persona 2: Chidi the Urban Customer

**Tagline:** The buyer who follows merchants, not brands — and hates jumping between apps to make a purchase happen.
**Demographic:** 31, male, Lagos (Yaba / Lekki axis), graduate, product designer at a startup, rides bikes and bolts, buys online weekly.
**Role:** Working professional and frequent consumer — the "customer" side of the graph.
**Tech Comfort:** High. Lives on multiple apps, uses transfers and cards, expects polished UX, and reads every new product skeptically.

### Goals
- Find sellers he can trust without a phone call — see who they are, what they sell, and proof they are real.
- Pay for things in the same place he found them, without screenshot-proof and "send to this account" rituals.
- Track what he ordered and when it arrives.
- Buy for family back home (Enugu) without coordinating three people and two apps.
- Keep his social life and his shopping in a rhythm that doesn't feel like admin.

### Frustrations
- Instagram discovery → WhatsApp negotiation → bank transfer → screenshot proof: four steps, each a place to get scammed.
- No way to see whether a seller is actually reputable beyond a follower count.
- Paying for delivery by sending more money to a "bike rider's" personal account.
- Everyone asks for his number, then spams him.
- Data and battery drain from notification-heavy apps.

### Behaviors
- Discovers products on Instagram and Twitter, verifies sellers by asking around in WhatsApp groups, pays by transfer, and screenshots everything "for the record."
- Uses multiple fintech apps but has no loyalty to any.
- Expects search to work — types what he wants, not a brand name.

### Scenario
Chidi wants running shoes after seeing a friend's post. Instead of the usual Instagram-to-WhatsApp-to-transfer dance, he opens Pamoja and types "running shoes" into search. Results come back grouped: business channels selling running shoes near him, plus people he knows who have posted about them. He opens a verified business channel, sees the catalog and prices, orders in chat, and pays in-app. The seller's channel shows delivery tracking; his order history lives in the same app as his chats. Later, when his sister in Enugu needs a phone case, he finds a vendor in Enugu through the same search and buys it for her — no third-party coordination.

## Persona 3: Ngozi the Diaspora Buyer

**Tagline:** The London-based daughter who is the family's bank, shopper, and tech support — and the proof that Pamoja is a corridor, not just an app.
**Demographic:** 36, female, based in London, nurse, Nigerian-born, sends money home monthly and buys goods for relatives in Enugu and Lagos.
**Role:** Cross-border buyer / remittance sender — the diaspora bridge.
**Tech Comfort:** Medium–High. Comfortable with UK apps and transfers, but frustrated by African-side fees, forex surprises, and unreliable delivery coordination.

### Goals
- Send money home cheaply and know it arrived — not a 3% corridor fee plus a phone call to confirm.
- Buy birthday gifts, school supplies, and household goods for relatives without depending on them to "go to the market."
- Trust that the vendor on the other end is real.
- Have one place for family chat, money, and shopping instead of WhatsApp + WorldRemitter + a bank app.
- Feel connected to home — see what her family and their town are up to.

### Frustrations
- Remittance fees and exchange-rate spreads she can't see until after the transfer.
- Coordinating delivery: "did the courier come? did Mama get it?" — pure WhatsApp chaos.
- Sellers abroad who won't accept her UK card, or demand "airtime" and other informal payments.
- No buyer protection — if the goods never arrive, the money is gone.
- Timezone: she is awake at 2am Lagos time and can't call anyone.

### Behaviors
- Sends money via transfer apps monthly, often same-day as her UK payday.
- Shops online for Nigeria using Instagram sellers and diaspora WhatsApp groups.
- Relies on her mother's neighbor as the "ground agent" for pickups and payments.
- Prefers apps that work in both pounds and naira without forcing her to juggle.

### Scenario
Ngozi's mother's birthday is next week. Instead of the usual plan (transfer money, call her aunt, ask a cousin to buy a wrapper, hope it fits), Ngozi opens Pamoja, searches "aso oke" and finds a verified business channel in Lagos that ships to Enugu. She orders, pays in naira from a wallet funded by her pounds, and the vendor's channel shows the order moving: packed, handed to courier, delivered. She sends her mother a birthday message in the same app. For the first time, the money, the shopping, and the family chat are one thing — and she can do it all at 2am her time because it is all async.

## Persona 4: Tunde the Creator-Merchant

**Tagline:** The early adopter who instantly gets the channel model — his channel is both his brand and his shop.
**Demographic:** 27, male, Lagos (Ikeja axis), self-taught sneaker reseller and content creator with 15k Instagram followers, side business in custom sneaker restoration.
**Role:** Hybrid creator-merchant — runs a social brand and a business from the same identity.
**Tech Comfort:** High. Early adopter, runs multiple business tools, experiments with new apps weekly, and is a natural evangelist.

### Goals
- Turn his following into paying customers without the Instagram-to-WhatsApp friction.
- Separate his personal social life from his public brand — but from one identity, not two apps.
- Show off his restoration work (social) and sell it (commerce) in the same place.
- Find suppliers and collaborators across Africa, not just Lagos.
- Be seen as a serious business so he can get wholesale terms.

### Frustrations
- Posting his work on Instagram drives followers, but every sale still has to go through DM negotiation.
- Keeping his personal WhatsApp separate from customer WhatsApp — he carries two numbers.
- No way for customers to see his catalog while browsing his content.
- Instagram's algorithm throttles posts with "too many links" — he can't put his shop anywhere.
- Getting paid for custom work requires deposits and trust he has to build from scratch with each stranger.

### Behaviors
- Posts daily on Instagram and WhatsApp status; treats his feed as his portfolio.
- Uses Google Forms for custom orders and collects deposits via transfer.
- Hangs out in sneakerhead and reseller communities across West Africa.
- Willing to try any tool that reduces his "DM-to-cash" time.

### Scenario
Tunde posts a before-and-after of a restored pair of Air Force 1s on his channel in Pamoja. Followers find him in search when they type "tunde soles" (his name) or "sneaker restoration" (his bio) — his channel comes up as a social result, and its business module shows his catalog of restored and resold sneakers. A buyer in Accra finds him through cross-border search, orders a restoration, and pays a deposit in-app. Tunde's personal feed stays for his friends; his channel is the public brand. He starts telling his Instagram followers, "add me on Pamoja — it's where I actually sell," and becomes the network's loudest recruiter.

## Anti-Personas

**The Large Enterprise** — a 200-employee manufacturer with ERP, procurement teams, and dedicated finance staff. Pamoja's peer-to-peer commerce, merchant toolkit, and chat-based ordering are built for owner-operators, not enterprise workflows. Serving them means building integration, roles, and procurement features that add weight to everything else. WeCom-style enterprise tools are documented in [[README]] as out of scope for now; Pamoja's merchant toolkit is not a lightweight ERP and should not pretend to be.

**The Fully Offline Rural User** — someone with no reliable data connection, no smartphone, and no bank access. Pamoja's core loop (channel search, in-app payment, social feed) requires connectivity, a device, and a funded wallet. The Africa-first architecture should keep the app data-light so it reaches far — but chasing a fully offline user requires agent networks and USSD channels that belong to a different product phase and a different cost structure.
